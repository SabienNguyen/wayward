    How the computer learned

    Each machine-learned opponent is a logistic-regression model that predicts who will win from the pieces on the board and in hand. It learned only from recorded games and their results, never from an engine's scores.

    Head-to-head results (12–16 games each, same 2-move search): Novice beat Newborn 12–0, Apprentice beat Novice 8–4, Veteran beat Apprentice 16–0. A model trained on 300 games scored worse than the 40-game one, so it was left out. More data doesn't always mean better play.

    The Classic levels use hand-tuned piece values and are still stronger than every learned model.

    The machine-learned opponents all search the same way. They differ only in how they judge a position, which each one learned by studying recorded games and who won them. More games studied means more experience.

    How shogi works

    Shogi is chess's Japanese cousin. Two players, 9×9 board, 20 pieces each. The big twist: pieces you capture change sides and can be dropped back onto the board as your own.

    1. The goal

    Checkmate the opponent's king: attack it so that no move can save it. There's no stalemate draw. A player with no legal move simply loses.

    Sente (▲, "black") moves first and sits at the bottom. Gote (△, "white") sits at the top. Both sides' pieces are the same colour; you tell them apart by which way the point faces.

    2. The pieces

    Tap any piece below to study it. Most pieces move mainly forward, so attacks go one way and defence matters.

    3. Capturing and drops
    • Every piece captures exactly the way it moves. Pawns capture straight ahead.
    • A captured piece goes onto your stand (komadai). It loses any promotion.
    • On your turn you can either move a piece or drop one from your stand onto any empty square.
    • Drops can give check, block a check, or fork two pieces. They're the heart of shogi tactics.
    4. Promotion
    • The promotion zone is the far three ranks (the opponent's starting area).
    • A piece may promote when it moves into, out of, or within that zone. You choose.
    • Promoted pieces flip over and show red characters. Silver, knight, lance and pawn become gold-movers. Rook becomes the Dragon, bishop becomes the Horse.
    • Kings and golds never promote. Dropped pieces always land unpromoted.
    • Promotion is forced when a piece would otherwise never move again: a pawn or lance on the last rank, a knight on the last two.
    5. Drop restrictions
    • Nifu (two pawns): you can't have two unpromoted pawns of your own in the same file.
    • Dead squares: no dropping a pawn or lance on the last rank, or a knight on the last two ranks.
    • Uchifuzume: a pawn drop may give check, but it may not give checkmate. Checkmating with a moved pawn is fine.
    6. Check, repetition and notation
    • When your king is attacked you must remove the threat: move the king, capture the attacker, or block it (a drop counts).
    • If the same position comes up four times, it's a draw (sennichite).
    • Notation: piece letter, then - move, x capture, * drop, the square, then + promote or = decline. Files run 9→1 from Sente's left; ranks a→i from the top. So P-7f pushes the pawn on file 7 to rank f.
